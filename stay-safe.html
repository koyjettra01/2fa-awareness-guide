Keep the extra protection working

Your codes are yours.
Keep them that way.

2FA helps, but scammers may still ask for the very thing protecting your account. Slow down before you approve or share anything.

Three habits to remember

  1. Keep codes private.

    Do not send a verification code to someone who calls or messages you. Enter it only on the real service's sign-in page for a sign-in you started.

  2. Do not approve a surprise.

    If Google asks about a sign-in you did not start, choose No. Go directly to your account's security settings to review activity; change your password if you suspect it was exposed.

  3. Keep recovery private, too.

    Protect backup codes like passwords. Never post them, send them to a helper, or leave them in a public project or screenshot.

Guidance: FTC account-protection advice and Google's sign-in prompt guidance.

Different methods, different protection

Know your options.

Use a method your account supports. Text codes add protection, but an authenticator app or a phishing-resistant option can be stronger.

Common verification methods and what to watch for
MethodHow it worksKeep in mind
Text-message codeA code arrives at your phone number.Attackers can hijack a phone number. A text can also lead you to a fake sign-in page.
Authenticator appAn app creates a short-lived sign-in code.It avoids the phone-number risk, but a scammer can still trick you into sharing its code.
Phone sign-in promptA notification asks you to approve a sign-in.Approve only a request you started. Choose No for an unexpected Google prompt.
Passkey or supported security keyYour device or key verifies the sign-in with the real service.Phishing-resistant options help protect against fake sign-in sites. Availability varies.

A passkey may replace the password-and-code flow. You do not always need two separate screens to get strong protection.
